# The framework

Six steps. Each answers one question and ends on something that can be
checked. Do not start a step while the one before it is unmet.

| Step | Question | Done when |
|---|---|---|
| Model | What happens, which lens shows it, and what are the things, what changes, and the beats? | the mechanism is one sentence; `model check` passes; from code, the person said yes |
| Message | What is the one claim? | the claim is one sentence and every beat has its caption |
| Form | Which picture is this subject's own? | three drafts of three different views, each stated in words before it was drawn |
| Grammar | Does it follow the house style? | the checklist in `review.md` passes |
| Time | What moves, when, and meaning what? | every beat names what moves and what that says |
| Deliver | Live, held, and still | the figure renders in the page, and its stills are written |

## Before the model: finding the picture

Not every subject is boxes with arrows between them. A device is parts
inside a case. A pipeline is work stepping through stages in time. A blur is
a number spread over a grid. `lenses.md` is the method for working out which
it is: four questions about the subject, then the lens that what is hidden
calls for, then how to draw, reduce, encode and step through it. Work through
it first. It ends with the two lines every model carries: the mechanism, and
the lens.

## Why the model comes first

Simulate, then draw. The picture is a view of a small model of the subject:
what exists, what is inside what, what passes or changes, how much, and in
what order the story is told.
Get that wrong and three well-drawn drafts are wrong three ways. Get it right
and the drafts differ only in form, which is the comparison worth making.

A model is small on purpose: two to nine things, two to seven beats. A system
with forty parts is not one figure. Pick the path through it that the claim is
about and leave the rest out, or draw a second figure.

## The explanation arc

Every figure says three things, each with a fixed home:

- **How it works**: the beats, in order, each with a caption.
- **Why it matters**: the claim, one sentence, under the figure the whole time.
- **What follows**: the implication, shown while the loop rests. Optional.

Concrete before abstract. A beat shows one thing happening to one named thing
("the API hands the job to a queue"), not a principle ("asynchronous
processing"). The principle is the claim, and it comes from the reader having
watched the concrete thing happen.

One idea per beat. If a caption needs "and then" twice, it is two beats.

## Two readers, one picture

The picture and its beats are the same for everyone. Each beat carries a plain
caption and, for a figure drawn from code, a technical one that names the real
file, function, or message. If the plain captions cannot explain the picture,
the picture is too dense: fix the picture, not the caption.

## What not to do

- Do not draw the org chart of the code. Draw what happens.
- Do not animate to decorate. Every motion says something about the system.
- Do not explain in the caption what the picture fails to show.
- Do not fill in a template. The plugin's studies are worked examples of
  technique. A subject's picture is designed from the subject, and most of
  the time it starts on the blank stage.
- Do not ship the first draft. The round of three exists because the first
  form that comes to mind is rarely the best one.
